Supplementary Note 1: Acoustic properties of super unit cells 
Firstly, we would like to demonstrate that the acoustic properties of a single super unit 
cell with a hard boundary on both sides are equal to those of a model consisting of two 
super unit cells arranged in  reverse. We conduct simulations on  the pressure field 
distributions of various models, as shown in Fig. S1. Model 1 represents a single super 
unit cell with hard boundaries on both sides, model 2 represents two super unit cells 
arranged in reverse with hard boundaries on both sides , and model 3 represents two 
super unit cells arranged periodically with hard boundaries on both sides. To note that 
unit cells with the same number represent same units. Comparing the pressure field 
distributions of the three models, t he pressure field distribution of model 1 is same as 
that of model 2 and different from that of model  3. This finding implies that model 1 
can be regarded as equivalent to model 2 instead of model 3 . Therefore, the acoustic 
properties of the PGUOM composed of a single super unit cell are not supposed to be 
the same as PGUOM multiple super unit cells.  
 
Fig. S1. Pressure field distribution of PGUOMs in different arrangements. (a) Pressure 
field distribution of a super unit cell with hard boundaries on both sides. (b) Pressure 
field distribution of  two super unit cells arranged in reverse. (c) Pressure field 
distribution of two super unit cells arranged periodically. 
  

Supplementary Note 2: Effect of amplitude and width of unit cell on the 
transmission of a PGUOM 
We present transmission color maps of varied amplitudes for the P GUOM with 
unevenly distributed unit cells, as shown in Fig. S 2. In this design, the phases of the 
unit cells form a phase gradient, while the amplitude and the width of each unit cell 
differ. We use “dL” to represent the width differences between unit cell or unit cell 3 
and unit cell 2. The blue area indicates a transmission is close to 0, signifying a better 
silencing effect, while the red area denotes a weaker silencing effect. According to Fig. 
S2, when dL is small, a better noise reduction occurs when the amplitudes of the unit 
cells are close. Conversely, when dL is large, a better noise reduction occurs when the 
amplitudes of the shorter-length unit cell significantly exceed those of the longer-length 
unit cells. 
In analysis section, we set the amplitudes of the three units to 1 , however, in the 
validation section, the usage of length-varying straight barriers revealed variations in 
amplitude within each unit cell.  Therefore, we investigated the impact of amplitude 
variations on the transmission of the  PGUOM. In Fig. S 3(a-f), we compared the 
pressure field distribution for different amplitudes of the transmission wave within each 
unit cell , noting  the amplitudes for the three -unit cells at the corner of each figure. 
Remarkably, when the amplitudes of the side unit cells differ (as seen in Fig. S 3(a, c, 
e)), the silencing effect is less pronounced. Conversely, when the amplitudes of the side 
unit cells are uniform (as shown in Fig.S3(b, d, f)), the silencing effect is more effective. 
Another difference between the analysis section and the validation section is the 
frequency of the transmission dip. Our PGUOM targeted a frequency of 2000 Hz, the 
observed frequencies of dips were at 1950 Hz and 2150 Hz. To address this, we 
examined the pressure field distribution at frequencies of 1950 Hz, 2000 Hz, and 2150 
Hz, as shown in Fig. S3(g-i). The pressure field distributions at 1950 Hz and 2150 Hz 
resembled the ideal pressure field distribution in Fig. S 3(b), suggesting that the 
mechanism of th e PGUOM remains effective. The frequency shift observed may be 
attributed to the inaccuracies in the height of the waveguide.

PGUOM. The amplitude of center units (A2) and side units (A1 and A3), ranging from 
0.1 to 1, while the phase gradient is maintained. Length differences between the center 
units and side units are set ranging from 0 to 0.8.

Supplementary Note 3: Cylindrical PGUOM with a target frequency at 2000 Hz 
To demonstrate the target frequency of the cylindrical PGUOM can be tuned to 2000 
Hz with a similar outer radius of the design , we exhibit the simulation results of the 
PGUOM with various patterns in Fig. S4 and Fig. S5. Fig. S4 uses the same pattern as 
we used in the paper, fan shape slits  for both unit cell 1 and unit cell 3. Fig. S 4 uses a 
different pattern, a circular shape slit is used for unit cell 1. Both patterns reduce the 
fabrication resolution requirements. The design process is similar as we did in the paper: 
we first adjust the degree/ radius of unit cell 1 and unit cell 3 of the PGUOM to achieve 
a phase distribution spanning 2𝜋 with a similar amplitude, as shown in Fig. S4(b-c) 
and Fig. S 5(b-c). Then, waveguides are added to achieve the transmission dip at the 
target frequency. Corresponding pressure field distributions and transmission spectra 
are plotted in Fig. S4(d-f) and Fig. S5(d-f). The simulation results demonstrate that the 
target frequency of the design is tunable, and the pattern can be optimized if the 
fabrication resolution cannot be satisfied.

Supplementary Note 4: Limitation of the cylindrical PGUOM measurement setup 
Fig. S6(a) illustrates the measurement setup for the cylindrical PGUOM, an impedance 
tube. Fig. S 6(b-c) compares experimental results (red lines)  and simulation results 
(black lines) for the abovementioned designs. Significant measurement errors become 
evident as the frequency exceeds 2000 Hz. To understand this phenomenon, we 
measured the transmission loss of an empty tube, as shown in Fig. S6(d). Ideally, the 
transmission loss should be consistently zero across all frequencies . H owever, the 
transmission loss becomes much more than zero when the frequency exceeds 2000 Hz. 
This observation strongly indicates that our measurement setup is insufficient when 
dealing with frequencies higher than 2000 Hz. As a result of this limitation,  the target 
frequency is adjusted to 1500 Hz and the dimensions of the design are refined for the 
validation of the design.

Comparison of experimental results (red lines) and simulation results (black lines) for

Supplementary Note 6: Thermoviscous losses effect on the transmission loss 
Fig. S8 presents a comparison of the simulated transmission loss with and without the 
inclusion of thermoviscous losses, at different openness levels. Thermoviscous losses 
primarily arise within the narrow air channels of the structure, especially near rigid 
boundaries. To assess their contribution, we conducted simulations both with and 
without thermoviscous effects. The results show that thermoviscous losses reduce the 
transmission peak and increase the transmission loss of the dip, but they barely shift the 
center frequencies. This suggests that thermoviscous absorption plays a secondary role 
in the overall silencing effect and is not the dominant mechanism in the PGUOM design.

thermoviscous losses at different openness levels. 
  

Supplementary Note 7: Experimental setup and validation of rectangular 
PGUOM 
In Fig. S 9(a), we present an image of the experimental setup for measuring the 
rectangular PGUOMs. Two microphones are employed to record the incident and 
transmitted sound pressure. A movable stage allows precise positioning of the 
microphones for measurement. Additionally, two plates and resin walls or foams are 
utilized to minimize acoustic wave losses and prevent noise interference from outside, 
ensuring the accuracy of our measurement results. To maintain the consistency of our 
simulations with experimental data, the models we built in the simulations are identical 
to the experimental setup, as depicted in Fig. S 9(b-c), representing t he pressure field 
distributions with rigid boundary conditions or PML boundary conditions. Although we 
used point speaker instead of using planar speaker. The distributions clearly indicate 
that the incident wave has transformed from a  point wave to a plane wave  before 
interacting with the design . Furthermore, both designs effectively reduce the pressure 
of the transmission field to nearly zero after the sound passes through them.
